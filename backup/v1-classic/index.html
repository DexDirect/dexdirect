Abstract

Scalable collection of dexterous manipulation demonstrations remains a major bottleneck for robot learning. High-fidelity interfaces often require costly hardware and extensive setup, while low-setup, low-cost alternatives tend to provide less precise control and impose greater cognitive workload on operators. We present DexDirect, a direct kinesthetic arm guidance for efficient dexterous demonstration collection. The operator drags a 6-DoF gravity-compensated robot arm directly by a handle, while a single webcam retargets operator’s other hand onto a 16 joints 13-DoF dexterous robot hand. User studies suggest DexDirect collects 17.2× and 3.2× more successful demonstrations compared to purely vision (AnyTeleop) and pose-tracking (TeleDex) baselines. An adapted NASA-TLX shows DexDirect greatly reduces mental demand, effort, and frustration, despite raising physical demand. A diffusion policy trained on DexDirect demonstrations reaches a 90% success rate on a cube pick-and-place task. These results suggest that direct kinesthetic arm guidance combined with vision-based hand retargeting provides an efficient low-setup and scalable interface for collecting dexterous manipulation demonstrations.

Method

Two hands, two control pathways

Arm motion needs global 6-DoF positioning over a large workspace and physical contact with the world. Finger articulation is high-dimensional but lives in a hand-local frame. DexDirect gives each the interface that suits it: kinesthetic guidance for the arm, webcam retargeting for the fingers. There is no leader arm, no operator-to-robot calibration and no arm-side retargeting.

Left hand

Direct kinesthetic arm guidance

  • The arm runs in a gravity-compensated drag mode with zero position stiffness and low joint damping, with the joint-level controller at 200 Hz.
  • A staged system identification (gravity direction, payload mass and center of mass, joint-wise torque scaling, Coulomb friction) corrects the model gravity torques.
  • Trajectories come straight from the joint encoders, with no pose estimation, retargeting, IK or Cartesian motion generation. Every recorded state was physically reached by the robot.
  • The rigid handle transmits contact forces, so the operator feels contact without a separate haptic device.

Right hand

Vision-based finger retargeting

  • A palm-facing webcam mounted 45 cm below the hand streams at 30 Hz into MediaPipe Hands, which returns 21 3D landmarks.
  • Wrist-relative landmarks are mapped to the 16-joint, 13-DoF MIDAS hand with the optimization-based dex-retargeting framework released with AnyTeleop.
  • No wrist pose is taken from the camera because the arm already sets it, so vision only handles hand-local finger articulation.
  • A 3D-printed forearm support keeps the palm centered in view and limits large wrist motion.

Evaluation tasks

Five contact-rich tasks

The tasks cover different arm-control demands: large-workspace positioning, sustained contact-force regulation, discrete precision contact and constrained extraction. Clips are real-time excerpts of DexDirect evaluation sessions. Numbers are user-study means.

Jenga extraction

Pinch the designated block and pull it fully out without collapsing the stack. This tests precise placement, stable fingertip contact and gradual force regulation.

94% success10.5 s per success4.8× faster than TeleDex

USB unplugging

Grasp the connector, align with its axis and pull until it fully leaves the socket. This tests a stable grasp and directed extraction force.

95% success5.9 s per success1.8× faster than TeleDex

Lazy Susan rotation & pick-and-place

Rotate the platform about 180° through direct contact to bring the cube close, then grasp it and place it in the target region without dropping it.

96% success8.4 s per success2.6× faster than TeleDex

Whiteboard erasing

Keep the eraser on the board while sweeping along it until the word “robot” is gone. This takes tangential motion and normal-force regulation at once.

91% success13.9 s per success2.8× faster than TeleDex

Keyboard typing

Type “bot” with every key registered in order and no wrong press. This tests repeated discrete contact with small targets.

71% success6.6 s per success2.7× faster than TeleDex

User study

Three interfaces, one robot

The robot, dexterous hand, finger-retargeting pipeline, visual feedback and task setups were identical across conditions. The interfaces differ only in how the operator commands the global motion of the robot hand.

DexDirect ours

Drag the physical arm by a wrist-mounted handle. Arm motion is read from the joint encoders, and a webcam handles the fingers only.

TeleDex

A handheld smartphone in a 3D-printed wrist mount is tracked in free space to command the arm. We used the authors’ open-source software and hardware.

AnyTeleop

An Intel RealSense RGB-D camera estimates the operator’s wrist pose. CuRobo’s receding-horizon MPC at 120 Hz turns it into joint trajectories.

Participants
10, with no prior experience on any of the three interfaces
Order
Counterbalanced across participants (ABC, BCA, CAB). All five tasks with one interface, then the next interface
Budget
1 min practice per task, then as many successful repetitions as possible in 120 s (180 s for Jenga)

Results

More demonstrations, less workload

DexDirect had the highest success rate on every task and also finished successful trials faster: 1.8–4.8× faster than TeleDex, and 3.0–5.6× faster than AnyTeleop on the tasks where AnyTeleop succeeded at all. The throughput gain therefore does not come from simply attempting more often.

Workload (adapted NASA-TLX)

DexDirect’s overall Raw TLX score was 2.01, compared with 3.08 for TeleDex and 3.81 for AnyTeleop. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed a significant reduction relative to both baselines (p = 0.0195 vs TeleDex, p = 0.00195 vs AnyTeleop).

  • Mental demand3.19→1.77
  • Frustration2.79→1.47
  • Effort3.40→2.17

TeleDex → DexDirect. The one trade-off is physical demand, which rises to 3.23 (vs 2.72 TeleDex, 2.62 AnyTeleop). DexDirect replaces the cognitive effort of tracking, coordinate remapping and visual correction with the physical effort of moving a gravity-compensated arm.

Policy learning

From demonstrations to an autonomous policy

A single operator collected 200 DexDirect demonstrations, separate from the user study, for cube pick-and-place: lift a cube from the table and drop it onto a box 30 cm away. The diffusion policy trained on them succeeds in 18 of 20 episodes (90%) with no operator intervention.

Limitations

What DexDirect does not do (yet)

  • Co-located by design. It needs a robot capable of safe, low-impedance gravity-compensated guidance, does not support remote teleoperation, and may be less ergonomic for larger, higher-inertia arms.
  • Two operator hands for one arm-hand system. Single-operator bimanual control would need each hand coupled to its own arm while its fingers are tracked. Visibility, ergonomics and safety of that design are open.
  • Finger control is still vision-based. It depends on monocular landmarks and morphology-dependent retargeting, and there is no distributed fingertip force feedback.
  • Scope of evaluation. The study covers ten participants, five short tasks, one platform and one policy task, and it measures the combined effect of direct coupling without isolating remapping, latency and contact cues.
